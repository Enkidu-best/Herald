Chapter One. The Wind Off the Bay

By evening the wind had shifted and began to pull in from the bay, carrying the smell of salt and wet stone. The lighthouse keeper closed the shutters, checked the wick, and sat down at the table where his tea was going cold in a tin mug.

In thirty years he had learned this sea by heart — not from charts, but by ear. A storm always began the same way: first the gulls fell silent, then the water changed its voice from a lapping to a dull knock, and only after that came the real wind.

Today the gulls had fallen silent at four in the afternoon.

He wrote it in the log in an even hand, added the date, and set down the pen. Less than an hour of daylight remained, which meant it was time to climb — two hundred and forty three steps that he knew as well as he knew the sea.

Chapter Two. Two Hundred and Forty Three Steps

The stairway wound up inside the tower in a tight spiral, and on every twelfth turn there was a narrow window. The keeper counted them out of habit, though he could have made the climb with his eyes shut.

On the hundred and eightieth step he stopped to catch his breath. Below, in the darkening water, something flashed — once, then again. Not a fish, and not a reflection: the light was steady, and far too white.

He narrowed his eyes, but could make nothing of it.

The lamp was waiting above, and it had to be lit before the last band of sunset went out. Everything else could wait until morning — or so he thought then, not yet knowing that morning would not be waiting at all.
